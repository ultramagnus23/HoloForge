\begin{abstract*}
% Trimmed to ~110 words to satisfy the Universal Template's ~100-word
% abstract limit (was ~220 words) -- content unchanged from the longer
% version, just compressed; every number stays macro-sourced.
Camera-in-the-loop correction is structurally unavailable for write-once
volume photopolymer recording media, since the reconstruction error is
measurable only after it is permanent. We replace it with a
differentiable digital twin of the NPDD recording chemistry inside the
hologram-design loop. Media-in-the-loop outperforms media-blind SGD at
every tested spatial frequency and contrast budget (mean gain
\MeanGainTwoX{}/\MeanGainFourX{}/\MeanGainEightX{}~dB at
$2\times/4\times/8\times$), without the compensation cliff small-signal
theory predicts -- a negative result, not an inconclusive one. A bounded
2D study confirms the gain survives beyond the 1D recording geometry. A
component-wise ablation identifies the medium's saturation response, not
its transport kinetics, as the binding physical constraint on achievable
gain. Code and results are released.
\end{abstract*}

\section{Introduction}
Phase-only CGH computes a modulation pattern whose diffracted field
approximates a target image. Camera-in-the-loop (CITL) optimization
closed the gap between the computed pattern and physical spatial light
modulator hardware by measuring the actual reconstruction and
backpropagating the residual into the display model~\cite{peng2020neural}.

This solution is structurally unavailable to an entire class of
holographic devices. Volume photopolymer media -- Bayfol HX~\cite{bruder2017bayfol},
PVA/acrylamide, PQ/PMMA~\cite{hsieh2022pqpmma} -- record a hologram as a
permanent refractive-index distribution formed by photopolymerization and
mass transport. The medium is write-once: measurement of the
reconstruction error is possible only after the error has become
unerasable. CITL's feedback signal arrives, by construction, too late.
Yet these media are precisely where model mismatch is most severe: the
recorded index profile is not a scaled copy of the exposure, but the
product of monomer diffusion~\cite{sheridan2012reply}, non-local polymer
chain growth~\cite{gleeson2008chain}, dye depletion, saturable index
response, and post-exposure shrinkage, each depending nonlinearly on the
exposure itself.

We close this gap by placing a differentiable model of the recording
chemistry -- not just the readout optics -- inside the design loop. This
work is the second in a two-part study; Part 1 characterized phase-only
CGH degradation under idealized-modulator assumptions using purely
objective image-quality metrics~\cite{tripathi2025holoforge}.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Model-mismatch correction for holographic displays.} CITL
optimization~\cite{peng2020neural} and its extensions correct the
display's forward model using camera measurements: gradient-based
Wirtinger optimization of the SLM phase~\cite{chakravarthula2019wirtinger},
learned proxy models for real-time photorealistic
reconstruction~\cite{shi2021towards}, and time-multiplexed control of
heavily-quantized modulators~\cite{choi2022time}. All require a
rewritable modulator observed live. Our setting inverts the
availability: the medium is write-once, so the model must carry the
full burden.

\textbf{Fabrication-aware surface-relief optics.} Neural
lithography~\cite{zheng2023neural} inserts a learned digital twin of a
lithographic process into differentiable design, but models surface
geometry -- an etched height map. Volume photopolymers differ in kind:
the recorded object is a volumetric index distribution produced by
chemistry, and readout is Bragg-selective.

\textbf{Concurrent work.} Wechsler et al.~\cite{wechsler2026shvam} couple
a differentiable wave-optical forward model to a photochemical model for
holographic volumetric 3D printing, optimizing time-multiplexed
phase-only holograms against a target dose distribution. The two settings
differ in what is recorded and how it is read out: their target is a
printed, threshold-converted solid, evaluated by dose distribution; ours
is a Bragg-selective index grating, evaluated by diffraction. Their
photochemical model includes inhibitor diffusion but not the non-local
production kernel central to NPDD, where polymer initiated at $x'$
deposits at $x$ -- a distinction in the physics, not only the
application. Their optimization target is dose-distribution shaping;
ours is the spatial-frequency-dependent compensation limit
Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff} tests.

\textbf{Adjoint/PDE-constrained inverse design.} More broadly, treating a
physical forward model as a differentiable layer inside a design loop is
established practice in nanophotonic inverse
design~\cite{hughes2018adjoint}, where adjoint methods compute the
gradient of a design objective with respect to many structural degrees of
freedom from two forward simulations. Media-in-the-loop holography is
this same paradigm applied to a recording chemistry's own governing PDE
rather than a static structure's electromagnetic response.

\textbf{Correction-limited by physics, elsewhere.} The compensation-cliff
hypothesis this paper tests -- that a linear pre-correction fails once the
correction it demands exceeds a physical budget -- has a conceptual
cousin in proximity-effect correction for electron-beam
lithography~\cite{li2015proximity}, where dose or shape correction for
electron scattering is itself bounded by the resist's own response and
by adjacent-feature interactions; the correction problem is well studied
there even though the physical mechanism (electron scattering vs.\
photopolymer index saturation) is unrelated.

Our novelty claim is scoped precisely: the first differentiable
\emph{recording} model for volume holographic media placed inside a
hologram-design loop -- not the first differentiable photochemistry
model in any loop.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
The twin couples the non-local polymerization-driven diffusion (NPDD)
reaction-diffusion system~\cite{sheridan2000nonlocal}, itself built on the
earlier local photopolymerization-driven-diffusion
model~\cite{zhao1994diffusion}, to volume diffraction via split-step beam
propagation, with gradients obtained by unrolling a spectral IMEX
integrator.

Four coupled mechanisms determine the recorded index profile from an
exposure $I(x)$. Local polymerization proceeds at a rate
$\kappa\,[I(x)\,d(x)]^\gamma$, set by the dose sensitivity $\kappa$ and
gated by $d(x)$, the fraction of unreacted photoinitiating dye remaining
at $x$ ($d$ decays by first-order bleaching at rate $k_{\text{bleach}}$,
so heavily-exposed regions lose sensitivity as recording proceeds). This
local production term is then spatially smeared by a Gaussian kernel of
width $\sigma$ before it is deposited as polymerized material: chains
initiated at $x'$ terminate and deposit at $x$, not at their point of
initiation, which is the physical content of ``non-local'' in NPDD. The
non-local term is therefore $G_\sigma * (F\cdot u)$ -- the kernel must
smear the full, already-formed production term $F\cdot u$, not the bare
monomer field $u$ before production acts on it, since it is the
newly-initiated chains that deposit non-locally, not the existing
monomer pool. Getting this backwards is a real failure mode, not a
hypothetical one: an earlier version of this codebase applied the kernel
to $u$ alone, which left the exposure pattern effectively unblurred and
silently disabled $\sigma$'s physical effect; it was caught because
non-locality sweeps came back implausibly flat at high spatial frequency,
and every result computed under the earlier form was retracted and
rerun once the kernel order was corrected. Monomer diffuses with an
effective diffusivity $D_{\text{eff}} = D_0 \exp(-\alpha_D N)$ that
itself falls as accumulated polymer $N$ builds up, coupling transport to
the recording's own progress. The accumulated polymer concentration
finally maps to a refractive-index modulation through a saturating
$\tanh$ response bounded by $\Delta n_{\max}$, the medium's physical
saturation ceiling -- the mechanism Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation}
identifies as the dominant constraint on achievable gain.

\section{Media-in-the-Loop Optimization}
Given target intensity $I_t$, we optimize the nonnegative, dose-budgeted
exposure $E(x)$ delivered to the medium, with the twin inside the loss.
Contrast headroom is enforced as a hard constraint
(\url{holomedia.optimize.contrast_project}), not merely
a nominal dose level: every optimized exposure is projected back onto the
feasible contrast set at every iteration, so the reported gains reflect a
solution that satisfies the budget throughout optimization, not only at
convergence.

\subsection{Baselines and oracle}
\label{sec:baselines}
We compare against three media-blind baselines and two oracles, all
under the identical exposure-domain constraints ($E \geq 0$, dose
budget, contrast cap) where applicable. \emph{Media-blind SGD} optimizes
assuming a linear, unsaturating medium ($\delta n \propto E$), then
evaluates the resulting exposure on the real twin -- the standard
practice this work argues against. \emph{Gerchberg-Saxton (GS)} performs
classic far-field phase retrieval, then converts the retrieved phase to
an exposure via the same naive linear assumption; closed-form, no
optimizer~\cite{gerchberg1972practical}. \emph{Linear pre-compensation (LPC)} is a closed-form
frequency-domain boost by $1/H(K)$ (the linearized recording transfer
function), capped at the contrast budget -- the direct realization of
the small-signal theory Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff} tests. The
\emph{constrained oracle} optimizes and evaluates against an ideal
linear medium under the same exposure-domain constraints as
media-in-the-loop; the \emph{unconstrained oracle} optimizes an index
profile $\delta n(x)$ directly against the ideal linear medium, with no
exposure-domain constraints, only the physical saturation bound
$|\delta n| \leq \delta n_{\max}$ (enforced via a soft $\tanh$ clamp).
Comparing the two oracles isolates the cost of the exposure-domain
constraints from the cost of the medium's own saturation physics; by
construction the unconstrained oracle's feasible set is a superset of
the constrained oracle's, so it can never score worse (verified as a
regression test, \url{tests/test_method_registry.py}).

\subsection{No compensation cliff}
\label{sec:no-cliff}
A small-signal analysis of the recording transfer function predicts a
compensation cliff: a spatial frequency $K_c(B_c)$ beyond which the
boost needed to pre-compensate the medium's rolloff exceeds the
available contrast headroom, so paired gain (media-in-the-loop minus
media-blind SGD) should cross from positive to negative near $K_c$.
Figure~F4 shows this is not what happens. Mean paired gain is
\MeanGainTwoX{}/\MeanGainFourX{}/\MeanGainEightX{}~dB at
$2\times/4\times/8\times$, and stays at or above zero at every one of the
15 tested $K$ -- the minimum observed value across all budgets is
\MinGainAllBudgets{}~dB (at $K=7.85$\,rad/\textmu m, $2\times$ budget,
where the 95\% CI does include zero; every other point is positive
outside its CI). Figure~\ref{fig:F5} makes the same point directly:
because gain never crosses zero, the K* estimators
(\texttt{find\_zero\_crossing\_K}, \texttt{find\_last\_crossing\_K})
return no crossing at any budget -- there is no observed $K^*$ to compare
against the predicted $K_c$
(\KcPredTwoX/\KcPredFourX/\KcPredEightX\,rad/\textmu m at
$2\times/4\times/8\times$). We report this as a negative result for the
small-signal cliff prediction, not as an inconclusive one: the data
spans the predicted $K_c$ at all three budgets with dense sampling and
still shows no sign change. Diffraction efficiency -- an independent
metric already computed alongside PSNR for every M1 job -- tells the same
story: mean paired gain of
\DEMeanGainTwoX/\DEMeanGainFourX/\DEMeanGainEightX\ percentage points at
$2\times/4\times/8\times$, rising with budget exactly as the PSNR gain
does, with \DENNegative\ negative value in \DENPairs\ paired comparisons
(\DEMinGainAllBudgets\ percentage points, effectively zero). The two
metrics do not diverge.

What the gain curve does show is a smooth decay from a large advantage
at low $K$ toward a small, non-zero plateau at high $K$, with the
plateau value increasing with budget. We interpret this via the
oracle comparison already in the pipeline: the gap between
media-in-the-loop and the saturation-respecting constrained oracle
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) narrows as $K$ increases, while the oracle's
own achievable gain over media-blind SGD falls. That is, the ceiling
itself collapses at high $K$, and media-in-the-loop tracks it down --
the ``cliff'' in the original hypothesis is real headroom loss, but it
is a property of what any exposure-domain-constrained method can
achieve, not a location where media-in-the-loop specifically fails.

Figure~R1 shows this directly rather than only in aggregate PSNR: target,
media-blind SGD, and media-in-the-loop reconstructions (1D transverse
intensity profiles, matching this paper's 1D recording geometry, not 2D
images) at the same sub-/near-/post-cliff $K$ used by M2, budget
$2\times$. At low $K$ media-in-the-loop visibly tracks the target's bars
while media-blind SGD barely modulates around a flat offset; at high $K$
both reconstructions degrade to comparable fine-fringe error, the same
ceiling collapse the gain curve shows numerically.

\subsection{Baseline completeness}
\label{sec:baseline-completeness}
Section~\ref{sec:baselines} defines media-blind GS and linear
pre-compensation (LPC) alongside SGD, but the results above compare only
against media-blind SGD. Figure~F4b closes that gap: paired gain of GS
and LPC over media-blind SGD, alongside media-in-the-loop, at budget
$2\times$. Both GS and LPC are frequently \emph{worse} than plain
media-blind SGD (negative paired gain) and swing erratically across $K$
-- GS ranges from \GSGainMin{} to \GSGainMax{}\,dB, LPC from
\LPCGainMin{} to \LPCGainMax{}\,dB, with no consistent trend.
Media-in-the-loop, by contrast, stays non-negative at every tested $K$ in
this comparison (minimum \MILGainMinAtTwoX{}\,dB), with its largest
advantage at low $K$ where GS and LPC are at their worst. LPC is the
direct closed-form realization of the small-signal compensation theory
Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff} tests; that it does not reliably beat even a
media-blind optimizer is itself evidence for the paper's thesis, not just
a missing baseline filled in -- linear pre-compensation is not a
sufficient substitute for optimizing through the medium's actual
nonlinear response.

\subsection{Is the compute worth it}
\label{sec:cost-benefit}
Media-in-the-loop's per-iteration cost is \ComputeMatchRatio$\times$
media-blind SGD's (Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff}'s M2 compute-matched
control; a full NPDD forward pass per iteration against one linear
multiply), and its mean advantage at the lowest budget is only
\MeanGainTwoX~dB. Whether that trade is worth making depends on what a
failed exposure costs. In computer-generated holography for a rewritable
spatial light modulator, a failed exposure costs one more display frame
-- compute is the only resource spent, and \ComputeMatchRatio$\times$ the
compute for \MeanGainTwoX~dB is a hard sell. Write-once photopolymer
recording is different in kind: every exposure consumes physical medium
that cannot be reused, and a media-blind exposure that turns out wrong is
not a wasted frame but a wasted, permanently degraded piece of material.
The relevant comparison for this setting is therefore compute cost
against wasted-media cost, not compute against compute -- and unlike
compute, physical medium does not get cheaper by waiting for faster
hardware. This reframing is specific to write-once recording; it is not
an argument for spending \ComputeMatchRatio$\times$ the compute in any
setting where a failed attempt is free to retry.

We measure ``wasted,'' rather than assert it. An exposure fails if its
PSNR falls more than \WastedMediaThreshDB\,dB below the constrained
oracle's ceiling at the same $K$ and budget -- a round number tied to the
oracle framework Section~\ref{sec:baselines} already defines for
isolating what is achievable from what a given method reaches, chosen
before looking at the result, not fit to it. Across the \WastedMediaNConfigs\
$(K,\text{budget})$ configurations in M1, \WastedMediaBSGDFrac\% of
media-blind SGD exposures fail this bar, against \WastedMediaMILFrac\%
of media-in-the-loop exposures. The finding is not fragile to the exact
threshold: at 1\,dB the rates are \WastedMediaBSGDFracOneDB\% vs.\
\WastedMediaMILFracOneDB\%, and at 3\,dB, \WastedMediaBSGDFracThreeDB\% vs.\
\WastedMediaMILFracThreeDB\%. Media-in-the-loop does not merely score a higher
mean PSNR; under this criterion, it essentially eliminates exposures that
would have to be discarded as unusable, which is the quantity write-once
recording actually pays for.

\subsection{Generalizing to 2D image targets}
\label{sec:2d-study}
Every result above uses this paper's 1D transverse recording geometry -- a
target varying in one spatial dimension, chosen so the recording-chemistry
compensation problem could be isolated from image-formation questions
specific to a full 2D hologram. To check that the same compensation
advantage survives that step up, we ran a bounded 2D study using the
identical NPDD/BPM physics generalized to a full 2D transverse grid (2D
non-local kernel, 2D split-step BPM readout;
\url{holomedia.npdd3d}, \url{holomedia.diffraction3d}), with no changes to
the governing equations.

We do not repeat the 15-point $K$-sweep: $K$ is not well-defined for a
broadband 2D image, so instead of one grating swept across spatial
frequency, we use \TwoDNTargets\ target images that vary in
spatial-frequency content instead -- a low-frequency disc, a
mid-frequency checkerboard (non-separable, unlike a product of two 1D
gratings, a genuine test of the 2D non-local kernel), and a multi-zone
resolution chart spanning coarse to fine bars in one image -- at
\TwoDGridN$\times$\TwoDGridN\ resolution, \TwoDNBudgets\ contrast budgets,
\TwoDNSeeds\ seeds, and the same \TwoDNIters\ optimizer iterations as the
1D headline grid.

Media-in-the-loop beats media-blind SGD in all
\TwoDNTargets$\times$\TwoDNBudgets\ target-budget combinations tested:
paired gain ranges from \TwoDMinGain\ to \TwoDMaxGain~dB across all seeds
and combinations, mean \TwoDMeanGain~dB -- comparable to or larger than
the 1D headline gains at the same budgets. Figure~\ref{fig:R2} shows the
images directly: target, media-blind SGD, and media-in-the-loop
reconstructions for each target. Seed-to-seed variance within a single
target-budget cell is small (Adam converges to visually near-identical
local optima across seeds at this resolution and iteration budget), so we
report the range across the full pooled set of paired seed-gains rather
than a per-cell confidence interval, which would overstate the precision
three seeds support.

This is a bounded check, not a repetition of the 1D study's statistical
design: \TwoDNTargets\ images is not a systematic sweep, and the physical
scope is otherwise unchanged (same medium defaults, same recording-chemistry
model, synthetic targets to avoid licensed-image-asset questions). What it
establishes is that the compensation advantage identified in the 1D
geometry is not an artifact of that geometry -- it survives a genuine,
non-separable 2D transverse recording problem.

\subsection{Physics-component ablation (S1)}
\label{sec:s1-ablation}
S1 isolates which NPDD mechanism drives the gain, by ablating one term
at a time ($\sigma\to0$ for non-locality, $D_0\to0$ for diffusion,
$k_{\text{bleach}}\to0$ for dye depletion, $\Delta n_{\max}\to100\times$
for an unsaturating linear-regime approximation -- see
\texttt{experiments/manifest.py}'s \texttt{S1\_CONDITIONS}) and
re-measuring paired gain at the same three $K$ used for M2, budget
$2\times$. Removing non-locality,
diffusion, or dye depletion individually leaves gain within
\SOneNoDiffusionRatio--\SOneNoNonlocalityRatio$\times$ of the
\SOneBaselineGain~dB baseline (diffusion removal reduces gain the most
of the three, to \SOneNoDiffusionRatio$\times$ baseline; the other two
are within 5\%). Removing the saturation nonlinearity is qualitatively
different: gain rises to \SOneNoSaturationGain~dB,
\SOneNoSaturationRatio$\times$ the baseline (Figure~F7). Saturation --
not non-locality, transport, or dye kinetics -- is the dominant physical
constraint on how much media-in-the-loop can recover, consistent with
the oracle-convergence picture in Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff}: it is
specifically the medium's saturating index response that caps the
achievable ceiling at high $K$.

\subsection{Parameter sensitivity (S2)}
S2 perturbs $D_0$, $\sigma$, and $\kappa$ independently by
$\{-50,-10,0,10,50\}\%$ at four $K$ spanning the collapse region
(4.60--5.60\,rad/\textmu m), budget $2\times$. Paired gain is
\STwoBaselineGain~dB at the unperturbed baseline and stays within
\STwoGainMin--\STwoGainMax~dB across every tested perturbation of every
parameter (Figure~\ref{fig:F8}) -- a narrow absolute
range, in sharp contrast to
S1's ablations above, where removing a mechanism entirely (not just
perturbing its value) changes gain by up to \SOneNoSaturationRatio$\times$.
The result is not sensitive to getting any single NPDD parameter wrong
by up to 50\%.
This is a one-at-a-time sensitivity design and does not capture
parameter interactions. The $\pm 50\%$ perturbation magnitude was chosen
as a round number, not derived from a measured literature spread, and it
still is not: Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref}'s literature fits
confirm one medium (Bayfol HX) in-regime, not the multiple independent
sources a real cross-source parameter spread would need, and attempting
one from two non-independent series of a single figure would overstate
precision the data does not support. Both limitations are carried
forward here explicitly rather than into a separate claim of robustness.

\section{Twin Validation}
\label{sec:twin-validation-ref}
We fit the twin against \NLiteratureSources{} digitized curves from two
independent, open-access sources: Bruder et
al.~\cite{bruder2017bayfol} (Bayfol HX, $\Delta n_1$ vs.\ exposure dose at
$K=8.98$\,rad/\textmu m, both their experimental and their own simulated
series -- inside this paper's own tested range of
1.96--15.71\,rad/\textmu m) and Hsieh et al.~\cite{hsieh2022pqpmma}
(PQ/PMMA, $\Delta n_1$ vs.\ exposure time at $K=24.94$\,rad/\textmu m --
outside that range, so an out-of-range check, not a same-regime one; this
curve is that source's own simulated output from their
independently-derived kinetic model, not a raw measurement).

An initial fit of $(\kappa, D_0)$ -- $\kappa$ (dose sensitivity) and $D_0$
(monomer diffusivity), every other \texttt{MediumParams} field held at its
literature-sourced default -- produced poor fits on all three curves
(NRMSE 0.68--0.87), which we did not accept at face value. A
self-consistency check (fitting the identical pipeline against a synthetic
curve generated by the twin itself, known parameters, 3\% noise) recovered
the known parameters to within 2\% at NRMSE 0.03, ruling out a fitting-code
bug. Diagnosis then isolated the actual cause: $D_0$ was the wrong second
free parameter. The Bayfol config's $\Delta n_{\max}$ (saturation ceiling)
had been cited from the source paper's Table~3, which its own text states
describes a \emph{separate} dye-screening experiment (\S4.4), not the
dosage-response condition Figure~3 actually reports -- a real citation
scoping error, not a modeling one. Refitting with $\Delta n_{\max}$ free
instead of $D_0$ dropped NRMSE from 0.73--0.87 to
\NRMSEBestInRegime--\NRMSEWorstInRegime\ on the two in-regime Bayfol series
(Figure~F2), each the best of 10 random-initialized fits (not a single
start, which a five-parameter NPDD forward model is not safe to trust).

Both in-regime fits clear NRMSE $<0.3$ (Figure~F2): the twin, with a
corrected saturation ceiling, reproduces Bayfol HX's rise-then-decline
$\Delta n_1$(dose) shape -- the earlier claim that the model's saturation
response is monotonic \emph{by construction} was wrong; it is the model's
own diffusive smoothing of the recorded fringe that lets $\Delta n_1$ fall
after a peak even while the local index modulation keeps saturating, and
correcting $\Delta n_{\max}$ let the fit reach that regime. One residual
inconsistency is reported directly, not smoothed over: the two Bayfol
series' fitted $\Delta n_{\max}$ values (same material, two exposure
intensities) disagree by a factor of \BayfolDnMaxRatio{}$\times$, which a
single shared saturation ceiling should not do. The PQ/PMMA fit
(NRMSE~0.10) is shown in Figure~F2 but excluded from this in-regime
conclusion for two reasons, not one: $K=24.94$\,rad/\textmu m sits
1.6$\times$ above this paper's own tested grid, and its digitized curve is
still rising, not yet saturated, at the full extent of the source's own
plotted data -- a still-rising curve cannot identify $\Delta n_{\max}$ at
all, so its low NRMSE reflects an under-constrained fit, not agreement.

This is a mechanism-level validation, not a claim of quantitative
agreement with either medium's absolute optical response: only one
independent medium family is confirmed in-regime (two series from one
Bayfol HX figure), $D_0$ and $\sigma$ were not independently re-audited to
their exact source condition the way $\Delta n_{\max}$ was, and M1--S2's
own headline claims are paired comparisons (media-in-the-loop minus
media-blind SGD, same twin for both arms), so they do not require the
twin's absolute response to match real photopolymers -- a systematic
offset or ceiling error cancels in the difference as long as it is common
to both arms, which it is by construction. What this validation
establishes is that the corrected twin reproduces the saturating,
rise-then-decline index response identified in S1 as the dominant
mechanism behind the paired-gain result, at parameter values consistent
with (if not fully pinned to) a real published medium. A
parameter-spread extraction across independent sources (to justify or
correct S2's $\pm50\%$ perturbation range) still is not attempted:
\texttt{results/summary/param\_spread.json} documents why one confirmed
in-regime medium is not enough for that claim. The two Optica-published
NPDD papers identified as primary sources (multi-$K$ growth-curve
families, the regime this model was actually designed against, and a
genuinely independent second medium family) remain paywalled; see
\texttt{data/literature/README.md} for what closing that gap needs.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
This study is simulation-only; its claims are conditioned on a physics
model (NPDD, Kogelnik coupled-wave theory~\cite{kogelnik1969coupled}, split-step BPM). A literature
overlay (Sec.~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref}) reproduces the saturating,
rise-then-decline index response of one in-regime medium (Bayfol HX,
NRMSE \NRMSEBestInRegime--\NRMSEWorstInRegime), after a citation-scoping
error in the medium's saturation ceiling was found and corrected --
reported as a mechanism-level validation, not quantitative agreement
across media, with the paired-comparison argument for why M1--S2's gain
claims do not depend on the twin's absolute fidelity regardless. Scalar diffraction bounds validity to moderate
numerical apertures; the RCWA validity-envelope grid (V3, formerly E7)
bounds this quantitatively for the $K$/$\Delta n$/polarization/slant
range tested -- readout-side error moves absolute PSNRs more than paired
gains, since the recording-side phenomena Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff}
examines (and finds no sharp cliff in, only a smooth ceiling collapse)
are, by construction of the paired comparison, upstream of readout.
The headline results (Sections~\ref{sec:no-cliff}--\ref{sec:baseline-completeness})
use 1D transverse recording kinetics: the target varies in one spatial
dimension $x$, isolating the recording-chemistry compensation problem from
image-formation questions specific to a full 2D hologram. This is a
deliberate scope choice, not a limitation of the twin itself -- the bounded
2D study above (Section~\ref{sec:2d-study}) uses the identical NPDD/BPM
equations generalized to a full 2D transverse grid and finds the same
positive-gain result on genuinely 2D, non-separable targets. What remains
1D regardless of transverse dimensionality is depth: the recorded index
modulation is treated as uniform through the slab depth at the recording
stage, and the model does not implement depth-resolved absorption (e.g.\
Beer--Lambert attenuation of exposure or dye concentration with depth);
depth ($z$) enters only at readout, via
extrusion of this single transverse profile through the split-step BPM,
with a depth-dependent lateral shift (not an amplitude change) from
shrinkage at slant. At the default medium (shrinkage
\ShrinkagePct\%, slant $\ShrinkageSlantDeg^\circ$, thickness
$\ShrinkageThicknessUm$\,\textmu m) that shift reaches
\ShrinkageShiftNm\,nm at full depth -- \ShrinkageFracLowK\% of a grating
period at the lowest tested $K$ and \ShrinkageFracHighK\% at the highest,
applied identically to both arms of every paired comparison. S1's physics
ablation is one-at-a-time and does not capture interactions between NPDD
mechanisms; S2's $\pm 50\%$ parameter-perturbation range remains a
round-number choice, not derivable from a cross-source literature spread
for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff}--\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref}'s
S2 discussion (one confirmed in-regime medium, not the multiple
independent sources that claim needs). Experimental two-beam closure (recording
optimized vs. naive exposures in a characterized medium and comparing
measured reconstructions) is explicitly future work.
