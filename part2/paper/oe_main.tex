\begin{abstract*}
% ~110 words, matching the Universal Template's abstract limit and the
% length discipline of the previous version. Ordering is deliberate (see
% the abstract-restructure pass): the failure-rate result leads, because
% it is the quantity write-once recording is actually billed in; the raw
% per-budget dB gains moved to the body, where they undersell nothing.
% Every number stays macro-sourced.
Camera-in-the-loop correction is structurally unavailable for write-once
volume photopolymer media: the reconstruction error becomes measurable
only once it is permanent. We replace it with a differentiable digital
twin of the NPDD recording chemistry inside the hologram-design loop.
Against a media-blind optimizer this cuts the fraction of exposures
falling more than \WastedMediaThreshDB\,dB short of the achievability
ceiling from \WastedMediaBSGDFrac\% to \WastedMediaMILFrac\% -- the
quantity write-once recording is billed in. The advantage holds on
non-separable 2D targets (mean \TwoDMeanGain~dB) and survives a 50\%
error in any single recording parameter. We find no sharp compensation
cliff, but a smooth ceiling collapse tracked by a saturation-respecting
oracle, and identify saturation, not transport kinetics, as the binding
physical constraint. Code and results are released.
\end{abstract*}

\section{Introduction}
Phase-only CGH computes a modulation pattern whose diffracted field
approximates a target image. Camera-in-the-loop (CITL) optimization
closed the gap between the computed pattern and physical spatial light
modulator hardware by measuring the actual reconstruction and
backpropagating the residual into the display model~\cite{peng2020neural}.

This solution is structurally unavailable to an entire class of
holographic devices. Volume photopolymer media -- Bayfol HX~\cite{bruder2017bayfol},
PVA/acrylamide, PQ/PMMA~\cite{hsieh2022pqpmma} -- record a hologram as a
permanent refractive-index distribution formed by photopolymerization and
mass transport. The medium is write-once: measurement of the
reconstruction error is possible only after the error has become
unerasable. CITL's feedback signal arrives, by construction, too late.
Yet these media are precisely where model mismatch is most severe: the
recorded index profile is not a scaled copy of the exposure, but the
product of monomer diffusion~\cite{sheridan2012reply}, non-local polymer
chain growth~\cite{gleeson2008chain}, dye depletion, saturable index
response, and post-exposure shrinkage, each depending nonlinearly on the
exposure itself.

We close this gap by placing a differentiable model of the recording
chemistry -- not just the readout optics -- inside the design loop.

This is a simulation study, and the twin it depends on is validated at
the level of mechanism rather than absolute optical response
(Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref}). That is a weaker guarantee than
it may appear to need, because of what the claims are: every headline
result is a \emph{paired} comparison in which both arms are designed
and scored against the same twin, so a systematic offset or ceiling
error in the twin cancels in the difference, by construction. What the
paired design does depend on is the twin's \emph{qualitative} response
being right -- the saturating, rise-then-decline index response --
which is what Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref} verifies against
published data. Independently, Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} shows the
result survives a 50\% error in any single recording parameter between
design time and recording time, which is a stronger and one-sided test:
there the error does not cancel.

This work is the second in a two-part study; Part 1 characterized phase-only
CGH degradation under idealized-modulator assumptions using purely
objective image-quality metrics~\cite{tripathi2025holoforge}.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Model-mismatch correction for holographic displays.} CITL
optimization~\cite{peng2020neural} and its extensions correct the
display's forward model using camera measurements: gradient-based
Wirtinger optimization of the SLM phase~\cite{chakravarthula2019wirtinger},
learned proxy models for real-time photorealistic
reconstruction~\cite{shi2021towards}, and time-multiplexed control of
heavily-quantized modulators~\cite{choi2022time}. All require a
rewritable modulator observed live. Our setting inverts the
availability: the medium is write-once, so the model must carry the
full burden.

\textbf{Fabrication-aware surface-relief optics.} Neural
lithography~\cite{zheng2023neural} inserts a learned digital twin of a
lithographic process into differentiable design, but models surface
geometry -- an etched height map. Volume photopolymers differ in kind:
the recorded object is a volumetric index distribution produced by
chemistry, and readout is Bragg-selective.

% not just the abstract: their model generates radicals pointwise,
% proportional to local light intensity, quenched by inhibitors at that
% same location before contributing to polymerization -- production is
% local. Their "chemical blur" is stated explicitly to come from ordinary

\textbf{Concurrent work.} Wechsler et al.~\cite{wechsler2026shvam} couple
a differentiable wave-optical forward model to a photochemical model for
holographic volumetric 3D printing, optimizing time-multiplexed
phase-only holograms against a target dose distribution -- a printed,
threshold-converted solid evaluated by dose distribution, with inhibitor
diffusion but not the non-local production kernel central to NPDD (where
polymer initiated at $x'$ deposits at $x$). Ours is a Bragg-selective
index grating evaluated by diffraction, testing the spatial-frequency-
dependent compensation limit of Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff}. The two
contributions are complementary, not competing, and we claim no
precedence: they demonstrate the paradigm experimentally, with physical
prints at the ten-micron scale, evidence this simulation study does not
offer. What this paper provides instead is the systematic
characterization -- a spatial-frequency sweep, a mechanism-wise
ablation, an achievability oracle, a compute-matched control, a
reduced-physics surrogate, a miscalibration study, and a failure-rate
quantification in the units write-once recording is actually billed in.

\textbf{Adjoint/PDE-constrained inverse design.} More broadly, treating a
physical forward model as a differentiable layer inside a design loop is
established practice in nanophotonic inverse
design~\cite{hughes2018adjoint}, where adjoint methods compute the
gradient of a design objective with respect to many structural degrees of
freedom from two forward simulations. Media-in-the-loop holography is
this same paradigm applied to a recording chemistry's own governing PDE
rather than a static structure's electromagnetic response.

\textbf{Correction-limited by physics, elsewhere.} The compensation-cliff
hypothesis this paper tests -- that a linear pre-correction fails once the
correction it demands exceeds a physical budget -- has a conceptual
cousin in proximity-effect correction for electron-beam
lithography~\cite{li2015proximity}, where dose or shape correction for
electron scattering is itself bounded by the resist's own response and
by adjacent-feature interactions; the correction problem is well studied
there even though the physical mechanism (electron scattering vs.\
photopolymer index saturation) is unrelated.

Our contribution claim is scoped accordingly, and deliberately not as
a priority claim: this is the first systematic characterization of
where, why, and how reliably chemistry-aware optimization helps for
Bragg-selective volume recording -- across spatial frequency, contrast
budget, physical mechanism, and twin miscalibration -- rather than a
claim to the first differentiable recording model in a design loop,
which the concurrent work above makes contestable and which is in any
case the less useful thing to have established.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
This section specifies the forward model in full: the exposure and its
feasible set (\ref{sec:model-exposure}), the recording chemistry
(\ref{sec:model-npdd}), the numerical integrator that produces the
gradient (\ref{sec:model-imex}), the readout optics
(\ref{sec:model-readout}), and the loss/optimizer that drives design
(\ref{sec:model-loss}). Table~\ref{tab:mediumparams} lists every physical
constant with its source. Reproducing this section is sufficient to
reimplement the twin; the released code (see Data availability) is the
reference implementation, not a separate description of it.

\subsection{Exposure and the feasible set}
\label{sec:model-exposure}
The design variable is the delivered exposure $E(x) \geq 0$, a
nonnegative intensity pattern over the transverse coordinate $x$. It is
constrained to a fixed mean dose $E_0$ and a contrast (peak-to-mean)
budget $B_c$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:feasible-set}
\mathcal{E}(E_0, B_c) = \Big\{\, E : x \mapsto \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} \;\Big|\;
\overline{E} = E_0,\;\; \frac{\max_x E(x)}{\overline{E}} \leq B_c \,\Big\}.
\end{equation}
$B_c$ is what any pre-compensating design can actually spend: boosting
one spatial-frequency component's exposure to counteract the medium's
rolloff draws down the headroom available everywhere else. Projection
onto $\mathcal{E}$ is neither a single Euclidean projection nor a purely
iterative one but an alternating clip-and-renormalize scheme
(\texttt{contrast\_project}), applied after \emph{every} optimizer step,
not only at convergence:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:contrast-project}
E &\leftarrow E \cdot \frac{E_0}{\overline{E} + \epsilon}
   &&\text{(dose projection)}\\
E &\leftarrow \min(E,\, B_c E_0)
   &&\text{(peak clip)}
\end{align}
iterated for 20 passes (dose projection, then peak clip, then dose
projection again). Clipping the peak removes mass, which perturbs the
mean the next dose-projection pass restores; the two constraints are not
satisfied simultaneously by a single pass, and convergence is geometric
in the number of passes (empirically $\sim\!2\times$ error reduction per
pass on typical intermediate exposures; 20 passes reaches
$\lesssim 10^{-6}$ relative error on the tested targets). Both operations
are elementwise and differentiable, so the projection is safe to place
inside the unrolled optimization loop, which is where every method in
this paper places it: exposures are re-projected onto $\mathcal{E}$ at
every iteration, not only reported after a final rescale.

\subsection{Recording: non-local polymerization-driven diffusion (NPDD)}
\label{sec:model-npdd}
Three coupled fields evolve on the transverse coordinate $x$ over the
exposure duration: free monomer $u(x,t)$ (normalized so $u(x,0)=1$),
immobile polymerized material $N(x,t)$ (with $N(x,0)=0$), and
unreacted photoinitiating dye $d(x,t)$ (normalized, $d(x,0)=1$). The
system~\cite{sheridan2000nonlocal}, built on the earlier local
photopolymerization-driven-diffusion model~\cite{zhao1994diffusion}, is
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}
   &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left( D_{\text{eff}}(N)\,
      \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \right) - G_\sigma * \big(F(x,t)\,u(x,t)\big),
   \label{eq:npdd-u}\\
\frac{\partial N}{\partial t} &= + G_\sigma * \big(F(x,t)\,u(x,t)\big),
   \label{eq:npdd-N}\\
\frac{\partial d}{\partial t} &= - k_{\text{bleach}}\, I(x)\, d(x,t),
   \label{eq:npdd-d}
\end{align}
with local initiation rate, non-local kernel, and effective diffusivity
\begin{align}
F(x,t) &= \kappa\,\big[I(x)\,d(x,t)\big]^\gamma, \label{eq:npdd-F}\\
G_\sigma(x) &= \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad
   \widehat{G_\sigma}(K) = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}K^2\sigma^2\right), \label{eq:npdd-G}\\
D_{\text{eff}}(N) &= D_0 \exp(-\alpha_D N). \label{eq:npdd-D}
\end{align}
The non-local kernel acts on the \emph{production term} $F \cdot u$, not
on the bare monomer field $u$ before production acts on it --
$G_\sigma * (F u)$, not $F \cdot (G_\sigma * u)$: a chain initiated at
$x'$ terminates and deposits polymer at $x$, so the smearing kernel must
act on where polymer is \emph{produced} (applying $G_\sigma$ to $u$
alone would leave $\sigma$ with no effect on recorded fringe contrast,
since $u$ is consumed pointwise regardless of where the kernel later
moves it -- Supplement S9). Monomer transport itself slows as polymer
accumulates, Eq.~\eqref{eq:npdd-D}, coupling diffusion to the
recording's own progress.

The recorded index modulation is a saturating function of accumulated
polymer, bounded by the medium's physical index budget $\Delta n_{\max}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:npdd-dn}
\Delta n(x) = \Delta n_{\max}\, \tanh\!\big(\beta\, N(x)\big), \qquad \beta = 1.5,
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is a fixed shape constant of the saturating map (not a
literature-sourced or fitted parameter; every fit in this paper --
Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref}, the SAT surrogate calibration --
treats $\beta$ as given and fits $\kappa$, $D_0$, or $\Delta n_{\max}$
against it). Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation}'s ablation identifies
$\Delta n_{\max}$-bounded saturation, not non-locality, diffusion, or dye
depletion, as the dominant constraint on achievable gain.

\subsection{Spectral IMEX integrator}
\label{sec:model-imex}
Equations~\eqref{eq:npdd-u}--\eqref{eq:npdd-d} are integrated over
$n_{\text{steps}}$ steps of size $\mathrm{d}t = t_{\text{total}} /
n_{\text{steps}}$ ($t_{\text{total}} = 10\,\mathrm{s}$,
$n_{\text{steps}} = 300$ throughout this paper) by a first-order
implicit-explicit (IMEX) splitting per step:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Explicit reaction half-step}: evaluate $F$
      (Eq.~\eqref{eq:npdd-F}), the non-local production
      $G_\sigma * (Fu)$ via one forward/inverse FFT pair using the
      precomputed kernel $\widehat{G_\sigma}(K)$, and update $u
      \mathrel{-}= \mathrm{d}t \cdot G_\sigma*(Fu)$, $N \mathrel{+}=
      \mathrm{d}t \cdot G_\sigma*(Fu)$, $d \mathrel{*}= \exp(-k_{\text{bleach}}
      I\, \mathrm{d}t)$ (exact for the linear bleaching ODE at fixed
      $I$); clamp $u \geq 0$.
\item \textbf{Implicit diffusion step}: with $D_{\text{eff}}$
      (Eq.~\eqref{eq:npdd-D}) evaluated at the post-reaction $N$ and
      spatially averaged to a scalar $\bar{D}$ per step, diffusion is
      applied \emph{exactly} in Fourier space,
      $u \leftarrow \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\big[\exp(-\bar{D} K^2\,
      \mathrm{d}t)\, \widehat{u}\big]$ -- unconditionally stable
      regardless of $\mathrm{d}t$, unlike an explicit diffusion update.
\end{enumerate}
Gradients with respect to the exposure $E(x) = I(x)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:npdd-F})
are obtained by unrolling all $n_{\text{steps}}$ and backpropagating
through the full trajectory (reverse-mode automatic differentiation
through the FFT-based operators above), i.e. the discrete adjoint of the
integrator just described, not a continuous adjoint of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:npdd-u}--\eqref{eq:npdd-d}. A checkpointed variant trades
recompute for memory (\texttt{forward\_checkpointed}); gradients from the
two are not bit-identical (measured cosine similarity
$\AblationCheckpointCosSim$ against the full unrolled gradient, vs.\
$\AblationSurrogateCosSim$ for a neural-surrogate gradient on the same
probe, Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation}'s supplement) because the FFT
recomputation inside checkpointing's backward pass does not retrace the
identical floating-point path, not because the adjoint itself differs.

\subsection{Readout: split-step beam propagation}
\label{sec:model-readout}
The recorded transverse profile $\Delta n(x)$ is extruded uniformly
through the slab thickness $T$ by default (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
states this scope precisely) and read out by scalar split-step beam
propagation (BPM) at readout wavelength $\lambda$ and background index
$n_0$: the slab is sliced into $n_z=32$ steps of thickness
$\mathrm{d}z = T/n_z$, each applying a transmission phase kick
$\exp\!\big(i k_0\, \Delta n(x, z)\, \mathrm{d}z_{\text{eff}}\big)$,
$k_0 = 2\pi/\lambda$, followed by band-limited angular-spectrum
propagation; after the slab, one angular-spectrum step propagates the
field to the reconstruction plane, intensity formed as $|E|^2$.
Post-exposure shrinkage $s$ detunes a fringe slanted at angle $\phi$ via
a depth-dependent \emph{lateral} shift, $\delta x(z) = s \tan(\phi)\, z$
(exact per-slice Fourier shift, $\mathrm{d}z_{\text{eff}} =
\mathrm{d}z\,(1-s)$ for longitudinal compression) -- a real but bounded
effect at this paper's default $20^\circ$ slant, quantified in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Kogelnik coupled-wave
theory~\cite{kogelnik1969coupled} is used only as closed-form validation
context, cross-checked against RCWA (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}); BPM
is the model inside every optimization loop and reported reconstruction,
covering the intermediate-diffraction regime some tested conditions fall
into (below).

\textbf{Diffraction regime.} The Klein--Cook parameter
$Q = 2\pi\lambda T / (n_0 \Lambda^2)$, $\Lambda = 2\pi/K$, separates
thin, intermediate, and thick (Bragg, $Q \gg 10$) volume-grating
regimes. At the default medium, $Q$ crosses 10 at
$K \approx 2.79\,\mathrm{rad}/\mu\mathrm{m}$: the two lowest tested $K$
points sit below it, in the intermediate regime, not deep Bragg --
precisely where Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff} finds media-in-the-loop's
largest gains, where a purely Kogelnik-regime intuition would least
apply and BPM's full treatment of non-Bragg orders is doing real work.

\subsection{Loss and optimizer}
\label{sec:model-loss}
The loss is scale-invariant MSE between the readout intensity $R = |E|^2$
(bpm output) and target intensity $I_t$: writing
$\alpha^\star = \langle R, I_t\rangle / \langle R, R\rangle$ (closed-form,
differentiable), the loss is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:si-mse}
\mathcal{L}(R, I_t) = \frac{1}{n_x}\big\| \alpha^\star R - I_t \big\|_2^2 \Big/ \max(I_t)^2,
\end{equation}
reported as $\mathrm{PSNR}_{\text{si}} = -10\log_{10}\mathcal{L}$. This
replaces an earlier max-normalized PSNR metric that was not the same
objective every optimizer minimized (a sum-normalized internal loss vs. a
max-normalized reported score); loss and metric are now the same function
up to a monotone transform, so an optimizer cannot minimize what it is
asked to minimize and still score worse on what is reported.
Optimization is Adam, learning rate $5\times 10^{-2}$, $n_{\text{iters}} =
800$ (M1/M2; other tiers as noted at first use), on a softplus
parameterization $E = \mathrm{softplus}(\theta) + 10^{-6}$ that enforces
nonnegativity without a hard constraint, with $\mathcal{E}$-projection
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:contrast-project}) applied after every step. The seed
enters \emph{only} at initialization: $\theta_0 = \epsilon\, z$, $z \sim
\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, $\epsilon = 10^{-2}$, drawn from a seeded generator;
the forward model, loss, and optimizer updates are otherwise
deterministic given $\theta$, so seed variation measures sensitivity to
starting point, not to any other source of randomness.

\section{Media-in-the-Loop Optimization}
Given target intensity $I_t$, we optimize the exposure $E(x)$ over the
feasible set $\mathcal{E}(E_0, B_c)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:feasible-set}), with
the twin (Section~\ref{sec:model-npdd}) inside the loss
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:si-mse}). What follows defines the baselines and oracles
compared against this optimization; the exposure parameterization,
projection, loss, and optimizer are all as specified in
Section~\ref{sec:model-loss} and used identically across every method
below unless stated otherwise.

\subsection{Baselines and oracle}
\label{sec:baselines}
We compare against three media-blind baselines, one reduced-physics
surrogate, and two oracles, all
under the identical exposure-domain constraints ($E \geq 0$, dose
budget, contrast cap) where applicable. \emph{Media-blind SGD} optimizes
assuming a linear, unsaturating medium ($\delta n \propto E$), then
evaluates the resulting exposure on the real twin -- the standard
practice this work argues against. \emph{Gerchberg-Saxton (GS)} performs
classic far-field phase retrieval, then converts the retrieved phase to
an exposure via the same naive linear assumption; closed-form, no
optimizer~\cite{gerchberg1972practical}. \emph{Linear pre-compensation (LPC)} is a closed-form
frequency-domain boost by $1/H(K)$ (the linearized recording transfer
function), capped at the contrast budget -- the direct realization of
the small-signal theory Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff} tests. The \emph{saturation-only surrogate} optimizes against a cheap
pointwise model that keeps the medium's saturating index response and
discards its transport physics entirely, then evaluates on the real twin
-- the control for whether the full recording PDE earns its cost
(Section~\ref{sec:baseline-completeness}). The
\emph{constrained oracle} optimizes and evaluates against an ideal
linear medium under the same exposure-domain constraints as
media-in-the-loop; the \emph{unconstrained oracle} optimizes an index
profile $\delta n(x)$ directly against the ideal linear medium, with no
exposure-domain constraints, only the physical saturation bound
$|\delta n| \leq \delta n_{\max}$ (enforced via a soft $\tanh$ clamp).
Comparing the two oracles isolates the cost of the exposure-domain
constraints from the cost of the medium's own saturation physics; by
construction the unconstrained oracle's feasible set is a superset of
the constrained oracle's, so it can never score worse (verified as a
regression test in the released code).

\section{Results}
Every number below is generated directly from the raw per-job result
data by an automated pipeline (see Data availability), never
hand-transcribed -- \SeedCount\ seeds per
iterative method, 15 spatial
frequencies $K$ spanning 1.96--15.71\,rad/\textmu m, three contrast
budgets $B_c \in \{2\times, 4\times, 8\times\}$.

\subsection{No sharp compensation cliff, but a smooth ceiling collapse}
\label{sec:no-cliff}
That the recorded index response departs from a scaled copy of the
exposure, in a way that depends on spatial frequency, is established for
diffusive photopolymers~\cite{kowalski2016design}. What is \emph{not}
established anywhere in that literature is where reshaping the exposure
stops being able to compensate for it. We therefore derive that
prediction ourselves and test it, rather than attributing it to anyone:
a small-signal analysis of the recording transfer function predicts a
compensation cliff, a spatial frequency $K_c(B_c)$ beyond which the
boost needed to pre-compensate the medium's rolloff exceeds the
available contrast headroom, so paired gain (media-in-the-loop minus
media-blind SGD) should cross from positive to negative near $K_c$.
Figure~\ref{fig:F4} shows this is not what happens. Mean paired gain is
\MeanGainTwoX{}/\MeanGainFourX{}/\MeanGainEightX{}~dB at
$2\times/4\times/8\times$, and stays at or above zero at every one of the
15 tested $K$ -- the minimum observed value across all budgets is
\MinGainAllBudgets{}~dB (at $K=7.85$\,rad/\textmu m, $2\times$ budget,
where the 95\% CI does include zero; every other point is positive
outside its CI). The same point holds directly under the K* estimators
(Supplement S1): because gain never crosses zero, neither returns a
crossing at any budget -- there is no observed $K^*$ to compare
against the predicted $K_c$
(\KcPredTwoX/\KcPredFourX/\KcPredEightX\,rad/\textmu m at
$2\times/4\times/8\times$; derivation and the self-consistent-vs-naive
$K_c$ definition in Supplement S1). We report this as a negative result for our own small-signal cliff
prediction, not as an inconclusive one: the data spans the predicted
$K_c$ at all three budgets with dense sampling and still shows no sign
change. The finding is methodological rather than a refutation of a
standing claim -- small-signal theory over-predicts the compensation
limit, and what the data shows instead is a smooth collapse of the
achievable ceiling, tracked by the saturation-respecting oracle
(below). Across the full grid the sign is unanimous: mean paired gain is
non-negative in all \WastedMediaNConfigs\ $(K,\text{budget})$ cells.
Under a null hypothesis of no effect, with each cell's sign an
independent fair coin, that outcome has probability
$2^{-\WastedMediaNConfigs}$. The cells are not fully independent -- they
share seeds and neighbouring $K$ -- so this is a consistency statement
about the direction of the effect rather than a calibrated $p$-value,
and the per-point confidence intervals above remain the quantitative
claim. Diffraction efficiency -- an independent
metric already computed alongside PSNR for every job in the sweep -- tells the same
story: mean paired gain of
\DEMeanGainTwoX/\DEMeanGainFourX/\DEMeanGainEightX\ percentage points at
$2\times/4\times/8\times$, rising with budget exactly as the PSNR gain
does, with \DENNegative\ negative value in \DENPairs\ paired comparisons
(\DEMinGainAllBudgets\ percentage points, effectively zero). The two
metrics do not diverge.

What the gain curve does show is a smooth decay from a large advantage
at low $K$ toward a small, non-zero plateau at high $K$, with the
plateau value increasing with budget. We interpret this via the
oracle comparison already in the pipeline: the gap between
media-in-the-loop and the saturation-respecting constrained oracle
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}) narrows as $K$ increases, while the oracle's
own achievable gain over media-blind SGD falls. That is, the ceiling
itself collapses at high $K$, and media-in-the-loop tracks it down --
the ``cliff'' in the original hypothesis is real headroom loss, but it
is a property of what any exposure-domain-constrained method can
achieve, not a location where media-in-the-loop specifically fails.
The distinction matters for what a designer should expect: not a
frequency past which chemistry-aware optimization stops paying, but a
progressively smaller prize that it continues to collect in full.

Figure~\ref{fig:R1} shows this directly rather than only in aggregate PSNR: target,

intensity profiles, matching this paper's 1D recording geometry, not 2D

compute-matched control (Section~\ref{sec:compute-matched}), budget
$2\times$. At low $K$ media-in-the-loop visibly tracks the target's bars
while media-blind SGD barely modulates around a flat offset; at high $K$
both reconstructions degrade to comparable fine-fringe error, the same
ceiling collapse the gain curve shows numerically.

The mechanism behind R1's outcome (exposure and recorded-index profiles,
Supplement S1) is direct: media-blind SGD's naive linear assumption
drives the exposure toward near-binary swings that, at high $K$, clip
$\Delta n(x)$ against $\Delta n_{\max}$ almost everywhere, wasting most
of the available contrast budget on an index modulation the medium
cannot deliver. Media-in-the-loop's optimized exposure stays inside the
medium's linear-to-saturating transition -- the same saturation
mechanism the component ablation (Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation})
identifies as the dominant constraint, visible here as an
exposure-domain trade-off rather than only an aggregate number.

\subsection{What write-once recording actually pays for}
\label{sec:failure-rate}
A mean PSNR gain is the wrong unit for this setting. What a write-once
medium charges for is not average quality but ruined material: an
exposure either yields a usable hologram or consumes a piece of film
that cannot be reused. So we measure the failure rate directly, and
measure ``wasted'' rather than asserting it. An exposure fails if its
PSNR falls more than \WastedMediaThreshDB\,dB below the constrained
oracle's ceiling at the same $K$ and budget -- a round number tied to the
oracle framework Section~\ref{sec:baselines} already defines for
isolating what is achievable from what a given method reaches, chosen
before looking at the result, not fit to it. Across the \WastedMediaNConfigs\
$(K,\text{budget})$ configurations in the headline sweep,
\WastedMediaBSGDFrac\% of
media-blind SGD exposures fail this bar, against \WastedMediaMILFrac\%
of media-in-the-loop exposures. The direction of the effect is stable across
thresholds, though its size is not, and the spread is worth stating
rather than summarizing: at \WastedMediaThreshDB\,dB the rates are
\WastedMediaBSGDFrac\% vs.\ \WastedMediaMILFrac\%, at 3\,dB
\WastedMediaBSGDFracThreeDB\% vs.\ \WastedMediaMILFracThreeDB\%, and
at 1\,dB \WastedMediaBSGDFracOneDB\% vs.\
\WastedMediaMILFracOneDB\%. Media-in-the-loop is better at every
threshold, but the gap narrows sharply at 1\,dB: a 1\,dB shortfall from
the ceiling is common for both methods, and it is the larger, more
consequential shortfalls that media-in-the-loop essentially
eliminates. Under this criterion media-in-the-loop does not merely score
a higher mean PSNR -- it essentially eliminates the exposures that would
have had to be thrown away.

Table~\ref{tab:failure-rate} extends the same measurement to every
non-oracle baseline defined in Section~\ref{sec:baselines}, including
the two added for this revision (RSGD, GPC). None of the added
baselines closes the gap to media-in-the-loop: RSGD's failure rate
tracks BSGD's closely at every threshold (expected, since the two
coincide exactly at two of the three budgets, Section~\ref{sec:baseline-completeness}),
and GPC's tracks LPC's closely, consistent with the same near-degeneracy.
The saturation-only surrogate (SAT) is the closest any cheap baseline
gets to media-in-the-loop by this measure at the two looser thresholds --
lowest failure rate of any baseline at 2\,dB and matching media-in-the-loop's
0\% at 3\,dB -- but at the stricter 1\,dB bar it fails as often as plain
media-blind SGD (\WastedMediaSATFracOneDB\% vs.\ \WastedMediaBSGDFracOneDB\%),
both well above media-in-the-loop's \WastedMediaMILFracOneDB\%. No baseline
overtakes media-in-the-loop at any threshold tested, and the closed-form
methods (GS, LPC, GPC) are consistently among the worst of the group at
1\,dB and 2\,dB, never better than any seeded optimizer at either
threshold.

\subsection{Compute-matched confound control}
\label{sec:compute-matched}
A compute-matched arm repeats the comparison at three representative $K$
(sub-/near-/post-cliff) with media-blind SGD given
\texttt{compute\_match\_ratio}$\times$ more optimizer iterations, so its
wall-clock/FLOP cost matches media-in-the-loop's full-NPDD-forward-pass
cost per iteration rather than its iteration count (full comparison
figure in Supplement S6). Gain remains positive in both arms at all
three $K$ and all three budgets; giving media-blind SGD proportionally
more compute does not close the gap. Media-in-the-loop's advantage is
not an iteration-budget artifact.

\subsection{Baseline completeness}
\label{sec:baseline-completeness}
Section~\ref{sec:baselines} defines media-blind GS and linear
pre-compensation (LPC) alongside SGD, but the results above compare only
against media-blind SGD. Figure~\ref{fig:F4b} closes that gap: paired
gain of GS, LPC, the saturation-only surrogate (SAT), a TV-regularized
SGD variant (RSGD), and a pointwise gamma pre-compensation baseline
(GPC), all over media-blind SGD, alongside media-in-the-loop, at budget
$2\times$. GS and LPC are frequently \emph{worse} than plain media-blind
SGD (GS: \GSGainMin{}--\GSGainMax{}\,dB; LPC: \LPCGainMin{}--\LPCGainMax{}\,dB,
no consistent trend); media-in-the-loop stays non-negative at every
tested $K$ (minimum \MILGainMinAtTwoX{}\,dB). LPC is the direct
closed-form realization of the small-signal theory Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff}
tests, so that it does not reliably beat even a media-blind optimizer is
itself evidence for the paper's thesis. RSGD tests whether media-blind
SGD's straw-man weakness is poor regularization rather than
media-blindness itself: its per-budget penalty weight is twin-tuned
(disclosed), and at the two lower budgets the tuned weight is exactly
zero -- RSGD is numerically identical to BSGD there (gain
\RSGDGainMin{}--\RSGDGainMax{}\,dB by construction); at $8\times$ gain
stays centered on zero, within the tuning grid's noise floor. GPC
inverts the medium's saturating map pointwise in real space (the
standard display-calibration ``gamma correction'' recipe) rather than
LPC's linearized frequency-domain inverse; its gain
(\GPCGainMin{}--\GPCGainMax{}\,dB) is numerically close to LPC's at low
$K$/heavy clipping (\GPCLPCMaxAbsDiffTwoX{}\,dB maximum difference) -- a
real degeneracy, not a coincidence: any monotonic pre-boost survives the
shared hard-clip step identically. Neither straw man rescues a
media-blind or pointwise-only approach.

\paragraph{Is the full recording model necessary?} Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation}
finds saturation dominant and the transport terms individually
non-critical, inviting the obvious economy: keep the saturating
nonlinearity, drop the PDE. The saturation-only surrogate (SAT) is the
exact closed-form limit of the twin with every transport term removed,
calibrated to the real twin by a one-parameter offline fit (NRMSE
\SATFitNRMSE), then optimized and evaluated exactly as every other
method. Calibrated, it is a real competitor, clearly better than the
media-blind baselines, but does not close the gap: it recovers
\SATFracOfMILTwoX\%, \SATFracOfMILFourX\% and \SATFracOfMILEightX\% of
media-in-the-loop's mean gain at $2\times/4\times/8\times$, averaged --
but that average conceals a strongly spatial-frequency-dependent
shortfall: \SATSubCliffFracMin--\SATSubCliffFracMax\% recovered at
sub-cliff $K=\SATSubCliffK$\,rad/\textmu m, falling to
\SATNearCliffFrac\% by $K=\SATNearCliffK$, and
\SATPostCliffFracMin--\SATPostCliffFracMax\% (sometimes failing to beat
media-blind SGD at all) past the cliff. At low spatial frequency the
medium's response is dominated by the pointwise saturating nonlinearity
alone; at high frequency the transport terms are exactly the mechanism
that smears the recorded fringe, so a model omitting them has nothing to
say about the regime where compensation is hardest -- making the
Section~\ref{sec:cost-benefit} compute argument evidence-backed and
conditional rather than asserted, and showing the transport terms carry
real joint information despite each being individually non-critical
(Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation}: that ablation measures redundancy
between mechanisms one at a time; this measures their joint
contribution once all three are removed together).

\subsection{Is the compute worth it}
\label{sec:cost-benefit}
Media-in-the-loop's per-iteration cost is \ComputeMatchRatio$\times$
media-blind SGD's (a full NPDD forward pass per iteration against one
linear multiply), and its mean advantage at the lowest budget is only
\MeanGainTwoX~dB. Whether that trade is worth making depends on what a
failed exposure costs. For a rewritable spatial light modulator, a
failed exposure costs one more display frame -- compute is the only
resource spent, and \ComputeMatchRatio$\times$ the compute for
\MeanGainTwoX~dB is a hard sell. Write-once photopolymer recording is
different in kind: every exposure consumes physical medium that cannot
be reused, so a media-blind exposure that turns out wrong is not a
wasted frame but permanently degraded material -- the relevant
comparison is compute cost against wasted-media cost, not compute
against compute, and physical medium does not get cheaper by waiting
for faster hardware. Nor is the choice binary: the saturation-only
surrogate of Section~\ref{sec:baseline-completeness} recovers a
substantial share of the gain at a small fraction of the cost, and is
the right answer where the medium is cheap enough that the full twin
cannot pay for itself.

\subsection{Generalizing to 2D image targets}
\label{sec:2d-study}
Every result above uses this paper's 1D transverse recording geometry -- a
target varying in one spatial dimension, chosen so the recording-chemistry
compensation problem could be isolated from image-formation questions
specific to a full 2D hologram. To check that the same compensation
advantage survives that step up, we ran a bounded 2D study using the
identical NPDD/BPM physics generalized to a full 2D transverse grid (2D
non-local kernel, 2D split-step BPM readout), with no changes to
the governing equations.

We do not repeat the 15-point $K$-sweep: $K$ is not well-defined for a
broadband 2D image, so instead of one grating swept across spatial
frequency, we use \TwoDNTargets\ target images that vary in
spatial-frequency content instead -- a low-frequency disc, a
mid-frequency checkerboard (non-separable, unlike a product of two 1D
gratings, a genuine test of the 2D non-local kernel), and a multi-zone
resolution chart spanning coarse to fine bars in one image -- at
\TwoDGridN$\times$\TwoDGridN\ resolution, \TwoDNBudgets\ contrast budgets,
\TwoDNSeeds\ seeds, and the same \TwoDNIters\ optimizer iterations as the
1D headline grid.

Media-in-the-loop beats media-blind SGD in all
\TwoDNTargets$\times$\TwoDNBudgets\ target-budget combinations tested:
paired gain ranges from \TwoDMinGain\ to \TwoDMaxGain~dB across all seeds
and combinations, mean \TwoDMeanGain~dB -- comparable to or larger than
the 1D headline gains at the same budgets. Figure~\ref{fig:R2} shows the
images directly: target, media-blind SGD, and media-in-the-loop
reconstructions for each target. Seed-to-seed variance within a single
target-budget cell is small (Adam converges to visually near-identical
local optima across seeds at this resolution and iteration budget), so we
report the range across the full pooled set of paired seed-gains rather
than a per-cell confidence interval, which would overstate the precision
three seeds support.

This is a bounded check, not a repetition of the 1D study's statistical
design: \TwoDNTargets\ images is not a systematic sweep, and the physical
scope is otherwise unchanged (same medium defaults, same recording-chemistry
model, synthetic targets to avoid licensed-image-asset questions). What it
establishes is that the compensation advantage identified in the 1D
geometry is not an artifact of that geometry -- it survives a genuine,
non-separable 2D transverse recording problem.

We also extend this study with the saturation-only surrogate (SAT,
Section~\ref{sec:baseline-completeness}), asking in 2D the same question
posed of it in 1D: is the cheap closed-form pointwise model enough on
its own? SAT's calibration is reused directly from the 1D fit rather
than re-derived (a property of the medium's dose-sensitivity curve, not
of whether the calibrating exposures were arranged in 1D or 2D).
Paired gain over media-blind SGD ranges from \TwoDSATMinGain\ to
\TwoDSATMaxGain~dB, mean \TwoDSATMeanGain~dB -- recovering
\TwoDSATFracOfMIL\% of media-in-the-loop's own mean gain on this study,
squarely inside the \SATFracOfMILTwoX--\SATFracOfMILEightX\% range the
1D headline grid reports across its three budgets. The cheap surrogate's
partial-but-incomplete competitiveness is therefore not a 1D artifact
either.

\subsection{Physics-component ablation}
\label{sec:s1-ablation}
A component ablation isolates which NPDD mechanism drives the gain,
removing one term at a time (non-locality, diffusion, dye depletion, or
the saturating nonlinearity) and re-measuring paired gain at the three
sub-/near-/post-cliff $K$, budget $2\times$ (full conditions and
per-condition figure in Supplement S2). Removing non-locality,
diffusion, or dye depletion individually leaves gain within
\SOneNoDiffusionRatio--\SOneNoNonlocalityRatio$\times$ of the
\SOneBaselineGain~dB baseline. Removing the saturation nonlinearity is
qualitatively different: gain rises to \SOneNoSaturationGain~dB,
\SOneNoSaturationRatio$\times$ the baseline. Saturation -- not
non-locality, transport, or dye kinetics -- is the dominant physical
constraint on how much media-in-the-loop can recover, consistent with
the oracle-convergence picture in Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff}: it is
specifically the medium's saturating index response that caps the
achievable ceiling at high $K$.

\subsection{Parameter sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
A sensitivity study perturbs $D_0$, $\sigma$, and $\kappa$ independently by
$\{-50,-10,0,10,50\}\%$ at four $K$ spanning the collapse region, budget
$2\times$ (full grid and per-parameter figure in Supplement S3). Paired
gain is \STwoBaselineGain~dB at the unperturbed baseline and stays
within \STwoGainMin--\STwoGainMax~dB across every tested perturbation of
every parameter -- a narrow absolute range, in sharp contrast to the
ablations above, where removing a mechanism entirely (not just
perturbing its value) changes gain by up to \SOneNoSaturationRatio$\times$.
What this does \emph{not} establish is robustness to getting a parameter
wrong: both arms are re-optimized on each perturbed medium here, so the
perturbation is common to both and cancels in the paired difference for
exactly the reason Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref} gives.
Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} runs the experiment that does test
miscalibration. The $\pm50\%$ perturbation magnitude is a round number,
not derived from a measured literature spread, for the reason
Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref} gives (one confirmed in-regime
medium, not the independent sources that claim needs).

\subsection{Robustness to twin miscalibration}
\label{sec:mismatch}
The practical question a write-once medium raises is not how the gain
varies across media, but what happens when the twin used at design time
is simply wrong about the medium that is later recorded. That requires
breaking the two arms apart in time: we optimize the exposure once
against the twin at its nominal parameters, then evaluate that same,
already-committed exposure on a twin whose parameters have been
perturbed, with no re-optimization. The exposure never learns it was
designed for the wrong medium -- which is the situation a practitioner
is actually in. Media-blind SGD needs no special handling as the control
arm: it never used a medium parameter in the first place, so it is the
arm that cannot be miscalibrated.

We perturb $D_0$, $\sigma$, $\kappa$ and $\Delta n_{\max}$ one at a
time by $\{\pm10, \pm25, \pm50\}\%$, at the same three
sub-/near-/post-cliff $K$ and budget $2\times$ used elsewhere (full
per-parameter curves in Supplement S4). Media-in-the-loop's advantage
survives the entire range: paired gain is \SThreeNominalGain~dB at
nominal and stays between \SThreeWorstWithinFifty\ and
\SThreeBestWithinFifty~dB across every tested perturbation of every
parameter, never approaching zero.

$\Delta n_{\max}$ is swept wider than the other three, because our own
fits disagree about Bayfol's $\Delta n_{\max}$ by a factor of
\BayfolDnMaxRatio{}$\times$ (Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref}), and
it is the one parameter where the advantage eventually breaks, one-sided:
gain is still \SThreeDnMaxAtMinusSixtyThree~dB under-estimating the
saturation ceiling by $-63\%$, but falls to \SThreeDnMaxAtHundred~dB at
$+100\%$ and goes negative, \SThreeDnMaxWorstGain~dB, at
$+\SThreeDnMaxFlipPct\%$. A twin that believes the medium can hold more
index modulation than it can will spend contrast budget driving an index
swing the real medium simply clips (the same saturation mechanism
Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation} identifies as dominant): $\Delta n_{\max}$
must not be over-estimated by more than about a factor of two.

\subsection{Joint miscalibration}
\label{sec:joint-mismatch}
Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} perturbs one NPDD parameter at a time, but a
real calibration error is never that clean -- every parameter is
uncertain simultaneously. We repeat the same design/evaluate split with
all four parameters ($D_0$, $\sigma$, $\kappa$, $\Delta n_{\max}$) drawn
independently per trial, Uniform($-$\SSixPctRange\%, $+$\SSixPctRange\%)
each, over \SSixNDraws\ independent Monte Carlo draws, reusing the exact
exposures Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} already optimized rather than
re-optimizing per draw. Paired gain stays positive on every single draw
(\SSixNNegative\ of \SSixNDraws\ draws negative), ranging from
\SSixWorstDrawGain\ to \SSixBestDrawGain~dB (mean \SSixMeanGain~dB, bootstrap
95\% CI [\SSixBootCILo, \SSixBootCIHi]~dB over draws). The one-parameter-at-a-
time robustness of Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} is not an artifact of
testing parameters in isolation: the advantage survives all four being
wrong together, at the same magnitude of error.

\subsection{Target-content and readout-noise robustness}
\label{sec:target-noise}
Every result up to this point measures the same periodic-bars target at
every $K$, so a skeptical reading is that the headline gain could be an
artifact of that one target family. We test this at a single
representative near-cliff $K$ across three genuinely different target
kinds (bars, sparse random spots, and uncorrelated random binary --
Supplement S8 for full values): the gain holds throughout, but varies
substantially in \emph{magnitude} by content -- largest for spots,
smallest (though still clearly positive) for random binary. The
advantage is real across target content, not a bars-specific artifact,
but its size is scene-dependent, a caveat any deployment should carry.

Separately, every prior result evaluates PSNR against a noiseless BPM
readout. We add a disclosed detector-noise model (Gaussian,
\SFiveNoiseStdPct\% of the reconstructed peak, applied identically to
every method's output before scoring) at the same representative
$K$/budget point: paired gain is \SFiveNoiselessGain~dB noiseless and
\SFiveNoisyGain~dB at \SFiveNoiseStdPct\% readout noise --
indistinguishable, indicating the advantage is not a fragile artifact of
an idealized noiseless readout.

\section{Twin Validation}
\label{sec:twin-validation-ref}
We fit the twin against \NLiteratureSources{} digitized curves from two
independent, open-access sources: Bruder et
al.~\cite{bruder2017bayfol} (Bayfol HX, $\Delta n_1$ vs.\ exposure dose at
$K=8.98$\,rad/\textmu m, both their experimental and their own simulated
series -- inside this paper's own tested range of
1.96--15.71\,rad/\textmu m) and Hsieh et al.~\cite{hsieh2022pqpmma}
(PQ/PMMA, $\Delta n_1$ vs.\ exposure time at $K=24.94$\,rad/\textmu m --
outside that range, so an out-of-range check, not a same-regime one; this
curve is that source's own simulated output from their
independently-derived kinetic model, not a raw measurement).

An initial fit of $(\kappa, D_0)$, every other \texttt{MediumParams} field
held at its literature-sourced default, fit all three curves poorly
(NRMSE 0.68--0.87). A self-consistency check (refitting a synthetic
curve generated by the twin itself, known parameters) recovered those
parameters to within 2\% at NRMSE 0.03, ruling out a fitting-code bug.
The cause was instead a citation-scoping error in the input: the
Bayfol config's $\Delta n_{\max}$ had been taken from the source's
Table~3, which its own text assigns to a \emph{separate} dye-screening
experiment, rather than the dosage-response condition its Figure~3
reports. Refitting with $\Delta n_{\max}$ free in place of $D_0$ dropped
NRMSE to \NRMSEBestInRegime--\NRMSEWorstInRegime\ on the two in-regime
Bayfol series (Figure~\ref{fig:F2}, best of 10 random-initialized fits).

Both in-regime fits clear NRMSE $<0.3$ (Figure~\ref{fig:F2}): the twin, with a
corrected saturation ceiling, reproduces Bayfol HX's rise-then-decline
$\Delta n_1$(dose) shape. That shape is not excluded by the saturating
index response, as one might expect: it is the model's own diffusive
smoothing of the recorded fringe that lets $\Delta n_1$ fall after a peak
while the local index modulation keeps saturating. One residual
inconsistency is reported directly, not smoothed over: the two Bayfol
series' fitted $\Delta n_{\max}$ values (same material, two exposure
intensities) disagree by a factor of \BayfolDnMaxRatio{}$\times$, which a
single shared saturation ceiling should not do. The PQ/PMMA fit
(NRMSE~0.10) is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:F2} but excluded from this in-regime
conclusion for two reasons, not one: $K=24.94$\,rad/\textmu m sits
1.6$\times$ above this paper's own tested grid, and its digitized curve is
still rising, not yet saturated, at the full extent of the source's own
plotted data -- a still-rising curve cannot identify $\Delta n_{\max}$ at
all, so its low NRMSE reflects an under-constrained fit, not agreement.

This is a mechanism-level validation, not a claim of quantitative
agreement with either medium's absolute optical response: only one
independent medium family is confirmed in-regime (two series from one
Bayfol HX figure), $D_0$ and $\sigma$ were not independently re-audited to
their exact source condition the way $\Delta n_{\max}$ was, and this
paper's own headline claims are paired comparisons (media-in-the-loop minus
media-blind SGD, same twin for both arms), so they do not require the
twin's absolute response to match real photopolymers -- a systematic
offset or ceiling error cancels in the difference as long as it is common
to both arms, which it is by construction. What this validation
establishes is that the corrected twin reproduces the saturating,
rise-then-decline index response identified in Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation}
as the dominant
mechanism behind the paired-gain result, at parameter values consistent
with (if not fully pinned to) a real published medium. A
parameter-spread extraction across independent sources (to justify or
correct the sensitivity study's $\pm50\%$ perturbation range) still is
not attempted, since one confirmed in-regime medium is not enough for
that claim (documented alongside the released code). The two
Optica-published NPDD papers identified as primary sources (multi-$K$
growth-curve families, the regime this model was actually designed
against, and a genuinely independent second medium family) remain
paywalled; the released code documents what closing that gap needs.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
This study is simulation-only; its claims are conditioned on a physics
model (NPDD, Kogelnik coupled-wave theory~\cite{kogelnik1969coupled},
split-step BPM), validated at the mechanism level against published data
in Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref}. Scalar
diffraction bounds validity to moderate numerical apertures; we quantify
this against rigorous coupled-wave analysis (RCWA) directly rather than
asserting a validity range, over a \RCWAVGridNCases-case grid spanning
$K$, $\Delta n$, polarization and slant (Supplement S5). Agreement is
close for unslanted gratings (deviation $\leq$ \RCWAUnslantedMaxDev) but
degrades sharply for the slanted $20^\circ$ geometry this paper's
default medium uses, reaching a maximum deviation of \RCWAVGridMaxDev\ at
$K=12$\,rad/\textmu m. This is evidence for, not against, the modeling
choice actually made: Kogelnik coupled-wave theory is used only as
closed-form validation context, never inside an optimization loop or a
reported reconstruction (Section~\ref{sec:model-readout}), and BPM's full
split-step treatment has no single assumed diffraction order to lose
track of the way Kogelnik does. Readout-side error of this kind moves
absolute PSNRs more than paired gains, since the recording-side
phenomena Section~\ref{sec:no-cliff} examines (and finds no sharp cliff
in, only a smooth ceiling collapse) are, by construction of the paired
comparison, upstream of readout.

The headline results (Sections~\ref{sec:no-cliff}--\ref{sec:baseline-completeness})
use 1D transverse recording kinetics, isolating the recording-chemistry
compensation problem from image-formation questions specific to a full
2D hologram -- a deliberate scope choice, not a limitation of the twin
itself, since the bounded 2D study (Section~\ref{sec:2d-study}) uses the
identical equations generalized to a full 2D grid and finds the same
positive-gain result. What remains 1D regardless of transverse
dimensionality is depth: the recorded index modulation is treated as
uniform through the slab depth by default; depth ($z$) otherwise enters
only at readout, via a depth-dependent lateral shift (not an amplitude
change) from shrinkage at slant -- \ShrinkageShiftNm\,nm at full depth
for this paper's default medium, applied identically to both arms of
every paired comparison.

The size of the error the uniform-depth assumption admits is set by the
film's optical density $\mathcal{D}$ at the recording wavelength: a
back-surface exposure of 89\%, 79\% and 50\% of the front-surface value
for $\mathcal{D} = 0.05$, $0.1$ and $0.3$ (weakly, borderline, and
strongly absorbing films, respectively, at the default thickness). We
test this directly rather than leave it purely parametric: extending the
NPDD recorder to attenuate the recording exposure with depth
(Beer--Lambert, independently re-recording at each of 16 depth slices)
and propagating the resulting depth-resolved index profile through the
BPM, the same exposures used in Section~\ref{sec:mismatch} give a paired
gain of \SSevenGainODZero~dB at $\mathcal{D}=0$ (reproducing the
uniform-depth result exactly, by construction), falling to
\SSevenGainODOneTenth~dB at $\mathcal{D}=0.1$ and
\SSevenGainODThreeTenths~dB at $\mathcal{D}=0.3$ -- narrowing by roughly
13\% but never closing, even at the strongly-dyed end of the range. Dye
bleaching is self-limiting and the gradient is common to both arms of
every paired comparison, which is why the effect size narrows rather
than the qualitative claim breaking. We did not measure $\mathcal{D}$
for the media modelled here; a practitioner can close it with a single
transmission measurement.

The physics
ablation (Section~\ref{sec:s1-ablation}) is one-at-a-time and does not
capture interactions between NPDD mechanisms; the
$\pm 50\%$ parameter-perturbation range of
Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} remains a round-number choice, not
derivable from a cross-source literature spread, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref} (one confirmed in-regime medium,
not the multiple independent sources that claim needs). Experimental two-beam closure (recording
optimized vs. naive exposures in a characterized medium and comparing
measured reconstructions) is explicitly future work.

\paragraph{Calibrating this twin for a new medium.} Our own fitting
experience (Section~\ref{sec:twin-validation-ref}) separates the
parameter costs cleanly (full guidance in Supplement S7). Two --
dose sensitivity $\kappa$ and saturation ceiling $\Delta n_{\max}$ -- are
identifiable from a single published dose-response growth curve
(NRMSE \NRMSEBestInRegime--\NRMSEWorstInRegime here). Two are not: $D_0$
and $\sigma$ need a multi-$K$ growth-curve family or an independent
measurement, since their signature is the spatial-frequency dependence
of the response, not the single-$K$ curve most sources publish. The
non-obvious lesson is provenance, not procedure: $\Delta n_{\max}$ must
come from the same dosage-response condition being fitted, not a
separate screening experiment at a different composition -- exactly the
citation-scoping error this section's own fit caught and corrected.
